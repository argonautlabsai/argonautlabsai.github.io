ARGODRIVE DS4 / DEEPSEEK V4.1 FLASH

DeepSeek V4.1 Flash at 111 tokens per second on two GPUs.

Two RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell · 192 GB of VRAM · 87 GiB of RAM in the VM · a 502 GB model file on NVMe.

DeepSeek V4.1 Flash is a mixture-of-experts model, and the 4-bit file of its October checkpoint is 502 GB. Our two graphics cards have 192 GB of VRAM between them. So most of the model’s experts stay on NVMe drives and are read when the model asks for them. On 22 November we measured what this machine does with a long prompt.

A 6,051-token prompt was read at 1,688 to 1,727 tokens per second. The JSON answer to it was written at 111 tokens per second with DeepSeek’s own drafter, and at 80 without it. The answer was the same, token for token, either way.

Reading and writing are different numbers.

A model does two things with a request. It reads the prompt, and then it writes the answer one token at a time. Reading goes fast because a whole prompt can be pushed through the layers together. Writing is slower because every new token needs the one before it.

Reading speed also grows with the length of the prompt. On the same machine a 512-token prompt reads at 371 to 387 tokens per second, a 1,556-token prompt at 730 to 741 and a 3,000-token prompt at 928 to 944. At 6,000 tokens it passes 1,300 on prose and 1,700 on the table.

Writing speed depends on what is being written. That is where the drafter comes in.

The machine.

It is one workstation with two RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell cards of 96 GB each. The engine runs in a Linux virtual machine with 87 GiB of RAM. The model file sits on a RAID0 of three NVMe drives, and a copy of its 289 GB of experts sits on another NVMe drive, which takes a share of the expert reads.

What the model uses most stays closer to the cards. The weights that are not experts live in VRAM, together with a cache of experts. Behind that cache, 55 GiB of RAM hold the next most used experts. Everything else is read from the drives when a token asks for it. The lists of what to preload were made from the server’s earlier requests, mostly JSON extraction and commentary on tables of figures, before the table prompt was written. That is the same kind of work the table prompt asks for.

From one card to two, in two steps.

One card wrote 21.6 to 21.9 tokens per second on our 512-token test. The first two-card build, on 18 November, split the model’s 40 layers between the cards, 20 on each, and gave each card its own cache of experts. That doubled decode, but one card always waited while the other worked.

The second build, on 21 November, puts both cards on every layer of every token. This is tensor parallelism. The attention is divided by heads, the other matrices by groups or output rows, and the cards exchange finished halves four times per layer. No value is ever summed across the cards, so every decode step has the logits of the one-card build. Exact kernel work added the rest.

A drafter that came with the model.

DeepSeek ships a small drafting network inside the V4.1 checkpoint. ds4 calls it DSpark. Its three stages guess up to five tokens ahead. The model then checks all of them in one pass over several rows, and every row is computed with the same arithmetic as ordinary one-token decoding. A guess that the model would not have written is thrown away. That is why the answer does not change.

The check is not free. Six rows cost about 53 ms, against 13 to 17 ms for one ordinary token. So the drafter only pays when most of its guesses are right. JSON is the good case: the structure repeats and the guesses land. On prose fewer guesses land, so the engine compares the drafter’s time with plain decoding as it goes and pauses it when it loses. On the novel it spends most of the answer paused, and its memory still costs about 5 % there.

What is checked, and what is not.

  • For the table prompt we compared the full logits of every position of the answer with and without the drafter. They were identical.
  • Without the drafter, the two-card build was checked against the one-card build on 21 November: every decode step had the same logits.
  • One of the three drafter runs on the table prompt read its prompt at 839 tokens per second, with the same expert reads as the other two. The range in the table leaves it out.
  • This is our build of DeepSeek’s open weights, not DeepSeek’s service. Equivalence to DeepSeek’s reference implementation has not been validated.

Run this benchmark.

The table prompt is synthetic: 67 days of invented weather readings for three cities, and a request for one city’s rows as JSON. A seeded script in the repository rebuilds it exactly, so anyone can run the same test.

python3 tools/deepseek-v41/make_weather_prompt.py 67 weather.txt
ds4-bench ... --chat-prompt-file weather.txt --ctx-start 6051 --ctx-max 6051 \
  --ctx-alloc 8192 --gen-tokens 600 --dspark --mtp-model <DSpark file>

Leave out the last two options for the row without the drafter. The full settings, the switches and the limits are in the README.

If you are writing about this.

You are welcome to. A few things will keep the piece accurate.

  • Give a speed its test and its date. 111 tokens per second is the 600-token JSON answer to the 6,051-token table prompt, with the drafter, on 22 November 2026. Without the drafter it is 80.
  • Reading is not writing. 1,700 tokens per second is how fast a 6,000-token prompt is read. It says nothing about how fast an answer appears.
  • The drafter figure is the good case. Prose was written at 65 to 69 tokens per second.
  • The two-card work came first, on another test. On the 512-token test one card decodes at 21.6 to 21.9 tokens per second, the layers split across two cards at 44.6 to 44.7, and both cards on every layer at 75.8 to 77.6. Do not compare these with the 111, which is the 6,051-token JSON test.
  • This is our build, not DeepSeek’s service.

Argonaut Labs ran DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, 4-bit, on one workstation with two 96 GB RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs, streaming the model’s experts from NVMe drives. On 22 November 2026, with the model’s own DSpark drafter, its CUDA port of Salvatore Sanfilippo’s ds4 read a 6,051-token prompt at 1,688 to 1,701 tokens per second and wrote a 600-token JSON answer at 111 tokens per second, against 80 without the drafter, with the same output token for token. On prose the drafter does not help: 65 to 69 tokens per second. Before the drafter, the two cards were made to work on every layer together with exact tensor parallelism: on a 512-token test, decode went from 21.6 to 21.9 tokens per second on one card to 75.8 to 77.6 on two.
